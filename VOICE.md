# VOICE.md: how Arriqaaq teaching posts are written

The writing contract for every teaching post: Usul al-Fiqh, the Mustalah Hadith
course, and any course after it. It comes from reading all 24 parts of
*Usul-Al-Fiqh Made Easy* in full, in order. Every quoted line below is the
author's own; the part number is given so it can be checked.

`DESIGN.md` governs how a post looks. This file governs how it sounds and how
it teaches. `.claude/skills/blog-voice/SKILL.md` covers frontmatter, directives
and Islamic conventions. When they overlap, DESIGN.md wins on visuals, this
file wins on voice and teaching method.

**The one-line version:** a patient fellow student who assumes you know
nothing, shows you the whole map first, builds each idea from the ground up,
tells you honestly where scholars differ and why, and never makes you feel slow
for asking.

---

## 1. The series has three registers, and the best writing is in the first

Reading the series in order, the voice changes. That matters, because "copy
the series" would copy the weakest stretches as readily as the strongest.

| Parts | Dates | Register | What it does best |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 to 7 | Dec 2023 to May 2024 | **Teacher in conversation.** Basmalah, a hook, scenarios, humour, reassurance, "hold on" detours. | The human voice. Hooks (Parts 1, 5), the cause-before-effect story (Part 2), fiqh vs usul as manual vs toolkit (Part 3), the witr scene (Part 5), first-principles framing (Part 7). |
| 8 to 15 | Jul 2024 to Jul 2025 | **Debate and evidence.** One-line epigraph, linguistic/technical definition, proponents vs opponents, each side's dalil, named-character "Practical Application". | Fairness to opposing views (Parts 10, 11), definitions as the root of disagreement (Part 11), worked everyday cases (Part 12), objectives before technique (Part 13). |
| 16 to 24 | Aug to Oct 2025 | **Class notes.** "In our ongoing study…", "in the next class". Every term gets Definition / Ruling / Example / table. | Exhaustive breakdowns and matrices (Part 18's four cases, Parts 21 and 22), the language-first foundation (Part 21), taking a live fiqh question as a case study (Parts 20, 22). |

What fades as the series goes on: the opening scenario, the direct "you", the
wry asides, the reassurance notes. By Parts 17 to 23 most posts open with a
recap paragraph and go straight to definitions. The content is more
complete; the human voice is thinner. **For new courses, aim for the Part 1 to
7 voice wrapped around the Part 16 to 24 completeness.** Hook and reassure like
Part 5, break down and tabulate like Part 18.

## 2. The voice, in detail

### 2.1 Who is speaking
- "We" for the blog and its community, "you" for the reader, "our Prophet (ﷺ)".
  A fellow student, not a pulpit: "we totally understand it" (Part 7), "Don't
  get discouraged" (Part 1).
- **Warm, plain, a little playful, never flippant about the deen.** "Hold up,
  how do scholars even come up with these answers?" (Part 1). "Surprising,
  right?" (Part 1). "Here's the deal:" (Part 5). The humour is in the framing;
  reverence for the texts and the imams stays whole.
- **Honest about difficulty.** "The terms 'fard' and 'wajib' sometimes swap
  hats, and it can leave you feeling lost in a word maze" (Part 5). Naming the
  confusion is the first half of explaining it.
- **Curious about the other side.** Part 10 asks a question on the opponents'
  behalf: "How do rejectors of Qiyas derive rulings in new cases?" Part 2 states
  the limits of its own criticism: "without any criticism aimed at any group."

### 2.2 The author's position, stated once and labelled
The series is evidence-first. Part 1: "It's not about following a specific
madhab, but rather understanding the fundamental principles." In Parts 8, 20,
23 and 24 this hardens into verdicts ("This position is considered weak,
because…", "has no basis in Islam"). That is the author's right; the rule is
**how** it is done:
1. Present each position with its holder and its dalil first.
2. Say which one the author finds stronger, **labelled as that**, with the
   numbered reasons (Part 20 does this well: "This position is considered
   weak, because 1… 2…").
3. Keep descriptions of groups sourced and measured. Part 24's "Examples of
   Severe Inter-Madhab Enmity" works because every line is quoted and cited; a
   bare label like "deviant" with no citation does not. For a first-principles
   course such as Mustalah, lean to the Part 2 and Part 10 temperature.

### 2.3 Sentence rhythm
Long, flowing, explanatory sentences broken by short ones that land the point:
"Remember, this was an era devoid of cars, flights, or trains." (Part 2). Bold
carries the one line a skimming reader must keep: "**It is obligatory to do
it, and sinful to neglect it.**" (Part 5). Never ten mid-length sentences in
a row.

### 2.4 Signature phrases (use naturally, don't sprinkle)
The author restates in plain words right after the technical statement:
- "Simply put, only Allah has the authority to make laws." (Part 1)
- "Let's break down the differences… in simpler terms" (Part 3)
- "So in essence:" followed by two bullets (Part 6, many times)
- "In simpler terms, Istishaab is maintaining the status quo." (Part 12)
- "To explain this simply, imagine a wealthy man who, before his death…"
  (Part 10, translating Ibn Hazm's dilemma)
- "In other words…", "Think of it as…", "Here's the deal:", "Hold on a moment;"
- "If you have not understood this, don't worry, we will explain it in detail
  next." (Part 9)

"Delve" and "journey" turn up often in the old posts. Don't carry them over.

### 2.5 Reassurance on rails
A `> **Note**:` that tells a beginner they may be lost, then names the lesson
that will pay it off (Part 1's note; "*we'll explain Qiyas later*" in Part 3).
Every forward reference needs a promise attached.

### 2.6 Humility about the field
"Scholars differed" and then the spread: "Some… Others… A third group…" (Part
19). "There is no precise minimum number agreed upon" (Part 7). "Note: This
issue is detailed and many other evidences are taken into account." (Part 22).
Never invent a consensus.

## 3. How a post teaches

### 3.1 Top-down, always: map, then regions, then streets
- **Series:** why the science exists (history, Parts 1 to 2) → what it is
  (Part 3) → raw material (hukm, 5 to 6) → where rulings come from (sources,
  7 to 15) → tools to extract them (16 to 22) → who may extract and who
  follows (23 to 24). Each post's closing line says why the next one follows.
- **Post:** the whole list before any item. Part 7 states "The sources of Hukm
  are the following: 1… 2… 3… 4…" then opens each. Part 5 shows the five
  categories of hukm taklifi, then splits fard four ways (time, extent,
  subject, object). Part 24 opens with an agenda in bold: definitions →
  perspectives → types → taqleed vs ittiba → prohibition → tamazhub.
- **Diagram first.** `[[SVG:name]]` goes at the top when one exists; it is the
  map.

### 3.2 First principles: start from something true outside the subject
The strongest examples start below the topic, in ordinary life:
- Part 21 (istinbaat) does not start with istinbaat. It starts with *how
  language carries meaning at all*: "when someone says 'tree', the sound
  doesn't naturally refer to a plant. It means that only because speakers of
  the language agree." Then "There's smoke" implies fire, "He stopped eating"
  implies he was eating. Then, and only then, mantooq and mafhoom.
- Part 13 (istislah) stops to say: "Before we can properly understand… we must
  take a step back and thoroughly examine the important objectives that Allah
  has established" (maqasid), then builds the topic on them.
- Part 7 states the method: "deconstructing complex problems… into their most
  fundamental elements."
- Part 1's chain: *only Allah legislates → it is in Quran and Sunnah → how did
  people take rulings from it → how did that become a science.*

Rule: before explaining term X, ask what the reader must already hold for X to
make sense, and supply it. Do not skip a link because it feels obvious.

### 3.3 The definition ladder (every new term)
1. **Root and plain meaning.** "*Usul* is the plural of *asl*, and it means
   whatever can have something else built upon it" (Parts 1, 3).
2. **The word in the Quran or hadith.** Tree of 14:24 for *asl*; Musa's du'a
   for *fiqh*.
3. **Technical meaning** (*Linguistically… Technically…*).
4. **An example the reader can picture.**
5. **What it is confused with, and the difference.** Fiqh vs usul vs qawaid
   (3); sabab vs illah, shart vs rukn, batil vs fasid (6); fard vs wajib (5);
   aam/khaas vs mutlaq/muqayyad via *afrad* (individuals) vs *awsaf*
   (attributes) (18); masalih mursalah vs bid'ah in six paired contrasts (13);
   taqleed vs ittiba (24).
6. **Divisions**, each with its own mini-ladder.
7. **A one-line summary or table.**

Define every unfamiliar word at first use, including small ones: *mukallaf*,
*nisab*, *altar*, *divining arrows* (Part 5). Short glosses in parentheses;
longer ones as a bullet.

### 3.4 Why before what
Cause first, then effect. Part 2 explains the Hijaz/Iraq split through seven
reasons (capital moved, Hijaz had the narrations, Iraq a melting pot, distance)
before any methodology table. A Google Maps screenshot and "no cars, flights or
trains" make the distance real.

### 3.5 When people disagree, check the definition first
Part 11's insight, worth repeating in every post that can use it: Istihsan has
four definitions; Shafi'i attacked the first, supporters never meant the first;
"the disagreement among scholars largely stems from different conceptions of
what Istihsan actually means." Likewise Part 24's *taqleed* has a technical
sense (so a layman asking a mufti is not taqleed) and a broad sense. Before
presenting "A says yes, B says no", ask whether A and B are saying yes and no
to the same thing.

### 3.6 Exhaustive breakdowns (MECE)
When a topic has combinations, enumerate every combination, then a summary
table. Part 18: ruling same/different × cause same/different gives four cases,
each with one Quranic example, a rule, an analysis, and a final matrix. Part 14
gives two independent classifications (by nature of the act, by probability of
harm). Use when a reader would otherwise ask "and what if…?"

### 3.7 Scholars' own words, then a plain restatement
Quote the scholar or text in a blockquote with a reference, then say it again
in the reader's language. Part 10 quotes Ibn Hazm's dilemma, then "To explain
this simply, imagine…", then two bullets of what each answer costs the
opponent.

### 3.8 Test the reader
Part 9: after the rule, give cases and ask "Is this correct qiyaas?" Show asl,
far', illah, hukm; ask; answer; give the **Reason**. Include wrong cases on
purpose (dog's skin, donkey vs horse, widow remarriage), so the boundary shows.
Use for any rule with conditions.

### 3.9 Practical application and case study
- **Practical Application** blocks with named people (Part 12): Mariam and the
  spill on her clothes; Ali buying a "trained" hunting dog from Zaid; Sarah
  claims a debt from Aisha; a hiker and a forest pool; bees on a picnic plate;
  kangaroo in Australia. Named, short, one rule each. Not "a person".
- **Case Study** for a live fiqh question the reader has met: beard (Part 20),
  touching women and wudhu (Part 22). Show the usuli rule, the positions by
  madhab with citations, then why they differ.

### 3.10 Close the loop
`## Conclusion`: one or two sentences saying what the reader can now do, then
"InshaAllah, in the next post we will…" with a link. Parts 8, 15 and 23 have no
teaser and end abruptly; don't repeat that. Part 11's closing paragraph (the
whole dispute is about definitions) and Part 15's (unlike rabbis and priests,
no person makes binding law) show a conclusion that adds one real insight
instead of repeating the post. Part 24, the last, says it is the last, links
the whole series and says what is next.

## 4. Anatomy of a teaching post

```
Frontmatter (see blog-voice skill)

[[SVG:the-map]]                           whole topic at a glance

*In the Name of Allah, The Most Gracious, The Most Merciful…*

> one quiet epigraph line (optional; Parts 4, 10, 11, 12)

HOOK  a scene or a question the reader has actually asked. 2 to 4 sentences.
RECAP "Last time we…" with a link. One sentence on where we are. Do not list
      every previous part with a gloss (Parts 14 to 16 do; it's a wall).
TODAY what this post answers, in the reader's words, as a short agenda.

> **Note**: reassurance for early terms, with the promise of where they land.

---
## <Plain heading, max 5 to 6 words>
   Definition ladder (3.3)
---
## Types / Divisions         list all first, then each
---
## How it differs from <neighbour>     table or A-vs-B
---
## Where scholars differ     positions, holders, dalil, why (3.5)
---
## Practical application / Case study / Test yourself
---
## Conclusion               recap + "InshaAllah, next…" link
```

Headings name the thing taught, never restate their first sentence, and carry
no emoji. For a term-by-term list (Parts 17 to 22 style) use a fixed card:
**Definition → Ruling → Example → Explanation**, then one summary table at the
end of the section, not after every item.

## 5. Mechanics

### 5.1 Language and spelling
- Casual transliteration, **no academic diacritics** (not ḥadīth, uṣūl, ʿilla).
  Parts 16 to 22 slip into diacritics; don't follow.
- One spelling per term for the whole course (section 8).
- Arabic text on its own line, then the translation, then the reference.
- (ﷺ) after the Prophet's name; rahimahullah / radiallahu anhu as the author
  writes them.
- InshaAllah and Alhamdulillah where natural, not as filler.

### 5.2 Citations
- Inline, at the end of the claim: "(Al-Milal wan Nihal 1/206)", "(Sahih
  al-Bukhari 4362)". Hadith link to sunnah.com where possible.
- Quran: "(Quran 2:185)".
- **Never invent or soften a quote, grading, narrator or page number.** If
  unsure: `<!-- TODO: citation -->` and tell the author.
- Every position has a holder: "al-Amidi says…", "according to the Hanafis…".

### 5.3 Formatting
- Bold: the term being defined, and the one line to remember. Bold-italic: the
  question in the reader's head ("***Now, will every thief have his hand cut
  off?***", Part 7).
- Italic: Arabic terms, book titles, the basmalah.
- Numbered lists when count or order matters; bullets for parallel items.
- **Markdown tables only.** The old posts paste raw `<html><head><style>` and
  inline-styled `<div><table>` blocks (and ChatGPT-export attributes like
  `data-start`); none of that belongs in new posts.
- Diagrams are `[[SVG:key]]`, portrait-first for 375px. No screenshots of
  diagrams.
- Paragraphs of two to five sentences. A phone screen is not a page.
- Do not paste internal class references ("Usool Fiqh 23", Part 22) or share
  links for another part (Part 18 ends with Part 17's tweet link) into a post.

### 5.4 Length
Median post is about 3,500 words; the ones that work best are short. Parts 3
(1,650), 14, 15 and 16 (1,400 to 2,000) say one thing clearly. Parts 5, 10, 21,
22 and 24 (4,500 to 5,400) each cover two or three lessons and could have been
two posts. **Target 1,500 to 2,500 words per post and split rather than
stuff.** One idea family per post.

## 6. What the author would never write

- A claim of fact the author did not supply or a source cannot back.
- Hype and brochure language: "a complex tapestry", "historic landscape",
  "woven into the very fabric of our literary DNA" (Parts 1, 3, 4),
  "stands as a testament", "serves as a guiding light", "This topic will
  engage your intellectual curiosity!" (Part 7), "meticulously elucidates".
- Leaked tool text, e.g. Part 7: "The transcript discusses the classification
  of hadith… Here's a detailed explanation based on the information provided."
- Padding in threes and fives: Part 5 repeats "Classification according to X
  refers to… Here are some examples of obligations falling under this
  classification:" four times with five near-identical bullets. Two sharp
  examples beat five blurry ones.
- Ranking one school above another by tone. Disagree on evidence, with names.
- Asking for money; shaming the reader for not knowing.
- A term used before it is defined or promised.
- A conclusion that restates the whole post.

### Known defects in the series
Fixed on the `mustalah-hadith-course` branch: the Harun ar-Rashid caliphate
(Part 2), the kalalah verse reference 4:176 (Part 23), the index link and
"Usul" typo (Part 11), the duplicated bullet (Part 3), the run-in Mandoob list
(Part 5), "aLeo" (Part 4), stray share links (Parts 7 and 18), leaked class
references (Part 22) and the "Ijithaad" and "definiton" typos. Do not copy the
old patterns, and re-check any post you touch for similar slips.

## 7. Pre-publish checklist

1. First screen: a real hook (scene or live question), not a paragraph about
   "the importance of knowledge".
2. Whole map (diagram or numbered list) before the parts.
3. Every term defined at first use: root → meaning → Quran/hadith usage →
   example → neighbour contrast.
4. At least one "but wait, what about…" moment, and one plain restatement
   ("In simpler terms…").
5. Does the post begin from something the reader already holds (3.2)?
6. Where scholars differ: positions, holders, dalil, and the reason for the
   difference; checked that they are disagreeing about the same definition.
7. Author's own view, if given, clearly labelled and after the fair hearing.
8. Every ayah, hadith, book reference real and complete, or TODO-marked.
9. Reassurance notes where a beginner might stall; forward references
   promised.
10. Practical application with named people, or a test-yourself, where the rule
    has conditions.
11. Rhythm varied; bold sparing; paragraphs short; no Section 6 phrases.
12. Spelling per section 8; no diacritics; (ﷺ) used.
13. Ends with a short recap, one real insight at most, and a linked teaser.
14. 375px and 1280px checked; `npx svelte-check`, `npm run build && npm run
    check:links` pass (CLAUDE.md).
15. Humanizer pass using two or three Part 1 to 7 passages as the sample.

## 9. The Mustalah Hadith course

**Source material** (in `references/`, git-ignored, never published):
- `Mustalah Hadeeth notes.pdf` (85 pages): the author's own class notes on a
  concise mustalah text by Shaykh Abdul Muhsin Al-Abbad and Shaykh Abdul Kareem
  Al-Murad. **The primary source for the course.** Its content, order and
  emphasis decide what each post says. It is organised by class (Class 1:
  history and early works of the science; Class 2: definitions, terminology,
  mutawatir; Class 3: ahad by routes, then maqbool and mardood; later classes
  continue), mixes slide headings with prose notes, and uses call-outs: "Why
  this book?", "Point of benefit:", "Note:".
- `mustalah-hadeeth_final-1.pdf` (80 pages): *Mustalah al-Hadeeth Made Easy*,
  Dr. Imaad Ali Jumah (Message of Islam), a chart-style book. **Support
  only**: check a definition, find a clearer example, fill a gap. Its order
  (introduction and terms → mutawatir and ahad by routes → accepted: sahih /
  hasan li-dhatihi and li-ghayrihi → rejected by isnad and by narrator → by
  ascription: qudsi, marfu', mawquf, maqtu' → other factors → narrators:
  conditions and jarh wa ta'dil → receiving and transmitting) is a handy
  cross-check but does not override the notes.

**Working rules**
- Substance comes from the notes and the author's brief for that post. If a
  point is in neither, ask; do not add.
- The notes' call-outs map onto this guide: "Point of benefit" becomes a
  `> **Note**` or a short bolded aside; "Why this book?" becomes the hook.
- The notes' Class 1 (history of the science) plays the role Parts 1 to 2 did
  for Usul. Open the course the same way: why this science exists, then what
  it is, then the tools.
- Series title: `Mustalah Hadith Made Easy (Part N) - Topic`, mirroring the
  Usul series. Confirm with the author before the first post goes live.
- First-principles order for mustalah: *a hadith has a chain and a text → who
  is in the chain → how many carry it → how reliable each is → how well
  connected → so what follows for accepting it.* Start Part 1 of the lessons
  from the plain idea "how do you know a report is true?" (cf. Part 21's
  language-first opening), then the vocabulary.
- Link back to the Usul series where topics meet (mutawatir and ahad in Part
  7 of Usul; khabar ahad vs qiyas in Part 2).
- Dense terminology (sahih, hasan, da'if, mursal, mu'allaq, munqati', …): the
  definition ladder, one chain diagram per concept (`[[SVG:…]]`, portrait-first),
  and one real hadith with a real citation. Mark the grade the way the notes do;
  never invent a grading.
- Where the two PDFs differ, say so and attribute each; don't silently choose.
